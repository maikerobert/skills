# Landing Page Standard

By Maike Robert. MIT License.

A landing page has one job: move one specific visitor one step further, to a signup, a trial, a purchase or a call. The platform, the beauty of the layout and the list of features come after that job. Profit is more sensitive to conversion than to cost: a better landing page pays off along the whole funnel, because every later step starts with more people.

Most landing page advice starts at the last link of the chain (button color, a new font). This standard works the chain in order, because each link depends on the one before it:

**buyer → awareness stage → promise → proof → objections → call to action → visual → speed → measurement**

Two modes: **build** a page (blocks 1 to 10, in order, with a gate after block 1) and **review** a page (the same blocks as a checklist, delivered as ranked findings).

## 1. The buyer and the stage, before any copy

- **Define the buyer by the buying situation, not by demographics.** What they are trying to get done, what they use today, what makes them switch, what makes them hesitate. Two people with the same age, income and city can want opposite things.
- **Find the awareness stage of the main traffic:** unaware of the problem, aware of the problem, aware of solutions, aware of this product, or ready to buy. The headline speaks to that stage; every section moves the visitor one stage forward.
- **Collect the visitor's own words.** Support questions, sales calls, reviews and complaints about competitors (app stores, review sites, communities), comments on the brand's posts. The words they use become the copy; their objections, in the order they appear, become the sections.
- **One goal per page.** One primary action. Every other action is visibly secondary, and the page has no links that take the visitor away before the decision (press, partners, social profiles in the hero).
- **Never clone a competitor's page.** A page that converts carries an invisible layer of research (vocabulary, ordered objections, chosen proof). Copying the layout copies none of it.

**Gate (build mode):** a one-screen message architecture with the buyer, the stage, the main promise, the proof inventory (what is real and available), the objections in order and the primary action. Nothing is written or designed before it is approved.

## 2. The first screen

The first screen decides whether the rest is read. It must answer, without scrolling: what this is, for whom, why it matters, and what to do now.

- **Headline:** a clear benefit or a clear pain in the visitor's words, short, specific and a little unexpected. Outcome over feature: not the storage size of the device, the number of songs in the pocket. If it names a pain, it promises the way out.
- **Subheadline:** makes the promise tangible, credible and feasible: how it works, for whom, in how long, at what cost of effort. It has room for two or three times the words of the headline, at a third of the size.
- **Primary call to action** with a short line beneath it that removes the main hesitation (free forever, no card, cancel anytime, takes two minutes).
- **Proof on the first screen:** one precise number, a recognizable name, a real face or a real screen of the product.
- **A real product visual** (the product in use, real screens, real customers), never a stock photo.
- **Five-second test:** show the first screen to someone from the target audience who has never seen it, hide it, and ask what the product is, who it is for and what they would do next. If they cannot say, rewrite before polishing anything else.

## 3. Proof equal to or greater than the promise

- **Build a pyramid:** strong and short at the top, denser and more technical as the page goes down. Every claim made at the top is demonstrated further down.
- **Proof sits next to the claim it supports,** not parked in a "testimonials" section at the end. There does not need to be a social proof section at all if every claim carries its own proof.
- **Kinds of proof, strongest first:** a demonstration (the product doing the thing), a result with a precise number, a named case, a testimonial with name, photo and result, the mechanism explained (how it works, the technology or partner behind it), recognizable logos.
- **Precise numbers persuade more than round ones** ("47,208 accounts" reads truer than "over 45 thousand"), but only numbers the business has approved for public use.
- **Real people over models.** Real customers and the real team convert better than stock faces. A rating of 4.6 with visible criticism is more credible than a perfect 5.

## 4. Sections that answer objections in order

- Each section answers the question the visitor has at that point of the scroll, in the order the research found. A typical order: what is it, will it work for me, how is it different, how much effort, how much money, can I trust it, what if it does not work.
- **Features become outcomes.** Each feature is written as what it lets the visitor do or stop worrying about.
- **Write for skimming:** a heading that carries the message on its own, then at most three short bullets or a demo. Depth for the technical buyer goes lower on the page or behind a link.
- **The FAQ is built from real objections** and real support questions, not from questions the team wishes people asked.
- **Show the real product in every section that describes it,** with real content, never lorem ipsum.

## 5. Calls to action

- **The label says what happens after the click** and what the visitor gets: a verb and an outcome ("Create my page for free", "Start my 7-day trial"). Never "Click here", "Submit" or "Learn more" as the primary action.
- **Repeat the primary action about every one and a half sections,** with the same destination. The wording can adapt to the stage of the scroll.
- **One risk-reducing line next to the button** (the click trigger), chosen from the most frequent hesitation.
- **The primary action dominates visually:** the strongest contrast on the screen, the only button in that style, within thumb reach on a phone. Secondary actions look secondary.
- **Test the message before the color.** A button color test rarely moves anything; a clearer label often does.

## 6. Pricing on the page

When the page shows prices:

- **Decide what the page sells:** the free signup or the paid plan. The plan the page wants to sell carries the strongest visual weight; with three plans, it sits in the middle.
- **Default to the choice you want:** the billing cycle preselected (usually annual, with the monthly equivalent and the saving shown), the target plan highlighted.
- **One axis of value between plans** (quantity or capability, not both), so the comparison is obvious.
- **Order the rows:** the feature that solves the main pain first, the routine items in the middle, the differentiator last, next to the button.
- **The full price up front.** No fee, tax or condition appears later in the flow.
- **Trial with or without a card is a business decision,** modeled with both numbers: volume of trials and conversion to paid.

## 7. The page does not end at the button

The signup flow is part of the landing page; most of the loss happens after the click.

- **Ask for the minimum.** Every field costs conversion; a phone field costs more than most. Ask for the rest after the first value.
- **Let the visitor reserve what they came for first** (the username, the address, the slot) and verify afterwards.
- **Taps over typing:** button groups instead of dropdowns for fewer than six options, paste allowed, password visibility toggle.
- **Right after signup, confirm the decision and show the next step,** so the first minutes lead to the first result.

## 8. The visual

- **Contrast with what the buyer sees every day.** Look at the sites and apps the audience uses and be distinct from them; memorable beats generic.
- **One point of attention per screen,** with the eye led by layout and images toward the primary action.
- **Real images or commissioned illustration,** never cheap stock. Good AI imagery is acceptable when it does not pretend to be a real customer.
- **A beautiful page is not the goal.** Pages that convert are often plain; judge by conversion, not by praise. The rest of the visual work follows the Interface Standard.

## 9. Speed

- **The platform does not matter; speed does.** Any builder or framework is fine if the page delivers the content fast.
- **Target:** grade A on GTmetrix, or good Core Web Vitals on a mid-range phone (largest contentful paint under 2.5 s, layout shift under 0.1, interaction under 200 ms). Optimize until it gets there.
- **Speed is conversion work,** not a separate technical task: measure it as part of the page's results, especially before paying for traffic.

## 10. Measure and iterate

- **One primary conversion event,** plus the steps before it: page view, primary action click, signup started, signup completed, trial or purchase.
- **Judge with the right traffic.** Warm traffic (the base, friends, followers) converts much better than cold traffic; only cold traffic shows the page's real ceiling.
- **One hypothesis at a time:** what changes, why, what result decides. Keep the change or revert it, and write down what was learned.
- **Plan for change.** The first version will be rewritten within weeks or months; build it so that copy and sections are easy to change.

## Review mode

Run the ten blocks as a checklist against the live page (desktop and a 390 px phone), a screenshot or a file, and deliver the findings in the ranked format of the Interface Standard (blocking, important, polish; what, why, fix; every finding names the block and rule it breaks), followed by what is working. Blocking means the page fails its job: the first screen does not say what it is, there is no clear primary action, the proof contradicts or falls short of the promise, or the signup flow breaks.

## Never

- Lorem ipsum, invented numbers, invented testimonials or a fake customer face.
- False scarcity, fake countdowns or invented stock limits. Real scarcity only.
- Dark patterns: hidden cancellation, preselected paid extras, guilt-tripping on the decline button.
- More than one primary goal on the page.
- Testing colors before the message, or redesigning before the buyer and the stage are clear.
- Cloning a competitor's layout.
